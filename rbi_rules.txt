Phishing links: Fraudsters build fake websites that copy a real bank, e-commerce site or search engine and spread the link via SMS, social media, email or instant messenger; victims who click without checking the URL enter their PIN, OTP or password into the fake site and hand it to the fraudster. Do not click unknown or unverified links; always type your bank's official website address yourself; check for https and a padlock symbol before entering credentials; check URLs for spelling errors; report suspicion to the local police or cybercrime branch.
Vishing calls: Callers or social-media contacts pose as bank staff, company executives, insurance agents, RBI officials or government officials, sometimes quoting your name or date of birth to sound credible, then pressure you under an invented urgency (blocking an unauthorised transaction, avoiding a penalty, an attractive discount, KYC update, unblocking your account or SIM, crediting a debited amount) into sharing your password, OTP, PIN or CVV. No bank, financial institution, RBI or genuine entity ever asks a customer to share username, password, card details, CVV or OTP over a call; never share these details with anyone, not even family or friends.
Frauds using online sales platforms: A "buyer" on an online marketplace (often claiming to be defence personnel posted far away) sends a UPI "collect/request money" request instead of paying, and insists the seller enter their UPI PIN to "receive" the payment; entering the PIN actually authorises an outgoing debit that transfers the seller's own money to the fraudster. There is never any need to enter a PIN or password to receive money; if an app asks you to enter a PIN to complete a transaction, you are about to send money, not receive it.
Frauds due to unknown or unverified mobile apps: Fraudsters circulate app-download links via SMS, email, social media or instant messenger that are made to look like a genuine bank or company app; once the customer installs the unverified app, the fraudster gains complete access to the device, including confidential stored data and OTP/SMS messages sent before or after installation. Never download an app from an unverified or unknown source or because an unknown caller told you to; check the publisher and user ratings before downloading; only grant an app the specific permissions it genuinely needs.
ATM card skimming: Fraudsters fit hidden skimming devices, dummy keypads or pinhole cameras on ATMs to copy card data and capture the PIN, or someone standing close by watches the PIN being entered; this data is used to clone the card and withdraw funds. Check the card slot and keypad for extra attachments before use; cover the keypad with your other hand while entering the PIN; never write the PIN on the card; never hand your card to anyone else for a withdrawal; ignore help offered by strangers at an ATM; if cash is not dispensed, press Cancel and wait for the home screen before leaving.
Frauds using screen-sharing apps or remote access: Fraudsters trick a customer into installing a screen-sharing or remote-access app (such as AnyDesk, TeamViewer or similar tools), then watch or control the device and use the visible banking/payment credentials to make unauthorised transfers. If you must install a screen-sharing app for a genuine technical issue, first log out of/deactivate all payment apps on the device; only install such apps when advised by the official toll-free number listed on the company's own website, never because a caller phoning from a personal number told you to; uninstall the app immediately once the work is done.
SIM swap or SIM cloning: Posing as telecom/mobile-network staff, fraudsters collect a customer's personal or identity details under the pretext of a free SIM upgrade or added benefits, then use those details to obtain a duplicate SIM (including e-SIM) for the victim's registered number and intercept the OTPs needed for unauthorised transactions. Never share identity credentials tied to your SIM card; if your phone loses network for an unusually long time in a normal environment, contact your mobile operator immediately to check whether a duplicate SIM has been issued.
Frauds by compromising credentials found through search engines: Fake customer-care numbers for banks, insurance companies or Aadhaar-update centres appear high in search-engine results though they do not belong to the real entity; customers who call these numbers are asked to share card details "for verification" and are defrauded. Always get customer-care numbers from the bank's or company's own official website, never from a number shown directly in search results; genuine customer-care numbers are never mobile numbers.
Scam through QR-code scan: Fraudsters contact customers under various pretexts and get them to scan a QR code using a payment app; scanning a QR code (like entering a UPI PIN) authorises an outgoing debit, so the customer unknowingly authorises money to leave their own account. Be cautious scanning any QR code with a payment app since it carries account-transfer details; never scan a QR code, enter a barcode, or enter mobile-banking PIN/mPIN/password in order to receive money — receiving funds never requires any of this.
Impersonation on social media: Fraudsters create fake profiles using a real user's photos and details, then message that person's friends asking for money for an urgent medical emergency or payment, or separately build trust with a target over time and use shared personal/private information to blackmail or extort them. Verify any money request from a "friend" or "relative" by calling or meeting them in person before you pay, since the profile may be impersonated; never pay unknown persons online; never share personal or confidential information on social media.
Juice jacking: A phone's charging port can also transfer data, so fraudsters plant malware on public charging ports/cables that infects any phone plugged in, giving them access to emails, SMS, saved passwords and other sensitive data. Avoid using public or unknown charging ports and cables.
Lottery fraud: Fraudsters email or call claiming the customer has won a large lottery and, to "release" it, ask for bank/card details on a website (which are then captured), or ask for taxes, forex charges, shipping or processing fees upfront — sometimes posing as RBI or a foreign bank/institution asking for a small transfer to unlock a much larger foreign-currency payout. Nobody gives away free money, especially large sums, so treat such offers as fraudulent; never pay money or share credentials in response to a lottery call or email; RBI never opens accounts for the public, never takes public deposits, and never asks the public for personal or bank details, so any message doing so in RBI's name is fake regardless of logos used; never respond to messages promising prize money, government aid or "KYC updation to release your prize."
Online job fraud: Fake job-search websites capture bank/card credentials entered at "registration," or fraudsters pose as officials of a reputed company, conduct a fake interview, then pressure the job seeker to pay for registration, a "mandatory" training program or equipment such as a laptop. For any job offer, including from abroad, first independently verify the identity and contact details of the employer; a genuine employer never asks a candidate to pay for being offered a job; never pay money on an unknown job-search website.
Money mules: Fraudsters convince a person via email or social media to receive stolen or illegal money into their own bank account in exchange for a commission, then direct them to forward it to another account (continuing a laundering chain) or to withdraw cash and hand it to someone; when the fraud surfaces, the account holder who received the money becomes the target of a police money-laundering investigation, even though they were duped. Never let someone else route money through your account for a fee; never respond to emails asking for your bank account details; do not be tempted by "easy commission" offers to receive and forward unauthorised money — if the source of funds cannot be proven legitimate, the receiver, not just the original fraudster, faces serious legal trouble.
Fake advertisements for loans (NBFC impersonation): Fraudsters place attractive fake loan ads (very low interest, easy repayment, no collateral) and email from addresses designed to look like senior officials of real NBFCs, then once a borrower is engaged, collect upfront "processing fee," GST, "intercity charge" or "advance EMI" and disappear without disbursing any loan; fraudulent websites are also built to surface in loan-related searches. A genuine NBFC or bank deducts its processing fee from the sanctioned loan amount — it is never demanded upfront in cash; never pay in advance or enter secure credentials for an online loan offer without independently verifying it through the lender's own official channel.
SMS, email, instant-messaging or call loan scams: Fraudsters send bulk messages using a real NBFC's logo as their profile picture to look credible, sometimes attaching fake Aadhaar/PAN or a fake NBFC ID card, then call and send fake sanction letters or fake cheque copies while demanding various "charges" before vanishing with the money once paid. Never trust an unsolicited loan offer received by phone, SMS or email; never pay or share financial credentials against such an offer without independently cross-checking it is genuine; never click links in unsolicited SMS/emails or reply to promotional messages; never open attachments or links from unknown senders.
OTP-based loan frauds: Posing as an NBFC, fraudsters message about a loan or a credit-limit increase and give a number to call; on calling, the customer is asked to fill a form capturing financial details, then talked into sharing an OTP or PIN, which is used to make unauthorised transfers from the account. Never share OTP, PIN or personal details with anyone in any form, including friends and family; regularly check your SMS/email to confirm no OTP was generated without your knowledge; only use a bank/NBFC/e-wallet's official website or branch to get product information or services.
Fake loan apps and fake loan websites: Unscrupulous loan apps offer instant, short-term loans, often at very high hidden interest rates, and use "limited period offer" pressure tactics to force quick, unconsidered decisions from borrowers. Verify the lender is registered with the relevant government regulator; check that a real physical address/contact exists; be suspicious if the lender seems more interested in your personal data than your credit score; remember no reputed NBFC or bank asks for payment before processing a loan application or lends without verifying your documents; independently verify that an NBFC-backed loan app is genuine.
Money circulation, Ponzi or Multi-Level-Marketing (MLM) scheme fraud: Chain-marketing or pyramid schemes promise quick money for enrolling more members, sometimes even paying the first few instalments to build gullible investors' confidence and attract more members by word of mouth; commission is paid for recruiting people rather than for selling any real product, and the scheme collapses once new recruitment slows, with the organisers disappearing with invested funds. Returns are proportional to risk, so a scheme consistently promising abnormally high returns (roughly 40-50 percent per year) is a fraud warning sign; any payment or bonus that is not tied to an actual sale of goods or services is suspicious; do not be lured by MLM/chain/pyramid schemes' promised high returns; accepting money under such money-circulation or pyramid structures is a cognizable offence under the Prize Chits and Money Circulation Schemes (Banning) Act, 1978, and should be reported immediately to the State Police.
Loans obtained with forged or stolen documents: Fraudsters steal a victim's identity documents and bank details, or pose as NBFC employees to directly collect a customer's KYC documents, then use these to fraudulently avail loans or credit in the victim's name. Exercise care before sharing KYC documents or an NACH form with anyone claiming to represent a bank/NBFC, especially individuals rather than an official channel; share such documents only with the entity's authorised personnel or official email IDs; follow up to confirm the entity has destroyed your documents if a loan application is not sanctioned or after a loan account is closed.
General digital-safety precautions: Be wary of suspicious pop-ups while browsing; always check for https and a padlock symbol before making any online payment; never share your PIN, password, card number or CVV with anyone — not your bank, not family, not friends; avoid saving card details on websites, devices or public/shared computers; enable two-factor authentication wherever it is offered; never open or respond to emails from unknown senders, which may carry malicious attachments or phishing links; never share copies of your chequebook or KYC documents with strangers.
Device and computer security: Change your passwords at regular intervals; keep antivirus software installed and updated on your devices; scan any unknown USB drive before use; never leave your device unlocked and configure auto-lock; never install unknown apps or software on your phone or laptop; never store passwords or confidential information directly on the device.
Safe internet browsing: Avoid unsecured, unsafe or unknown websites and unknown browsers; never save passwords or enter secure credentials on a public or shared device; never share private information with unknown persons on social media; always confirm https and a padlock symbol on any page you are redirected to from an email or SMS link before entering anything sensitive.
Safe internet banking: Use the on-screen virtual keyboard on public devices since physical keystrokes can be captured; always log out of an internet-banking session immediately after use; update passwords periodically; never reuse the same password for email and internet banking; avoid doing financial transactions from public terminals such as cyber cafés.
Signs that a phone may be under surveillance (spyware): unfamiliar apps appearing that you did not install; the battery draining faster than usual; the phone overheating for no clear reason; a sudden unexplained spike in data usage; the phone failing to shut down properly or taking unusually long to power off; unexplained or unrecognised text-message activity, since spyware can use SMS to send and receive data in the background.
Immediate actions after a fraud occurs: block the debit/credit card and also ask the bank to freeze debits on the linked account, either at the branch or via the bank's official customer-care number listed on its own website; also check and secure other channels such as net banking and mobile banking so the fraud cannot continue there; call the national helpline 1930 (or 155260) or file a complaint on the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal at cybercrime.gov.in; if the fraud stemmed from a data leak on the phone, reset the device to factory settings (Settings > Reset > Factory Data Reset).
Debit/credit card safety habits: Deactivate features you are not using — such as online/international transactions and Near-Field-Communication (NFC/contactless) — and re-enable them only when needed; always check the amount shown on the POS machine or NFC reader before entering your PIN or tapping; never let a merchant take your card out of your sight to swipe it; always cover the keypad with your other hand while entering the PIN at a POS terminal or ATM.
Email account security: Never click links in emails from unknown senders; avoid opening email on public or free/shared networks; never store your secure credentials or bank passwords inside an email account.
Password security: Use a mix of letters, numbers and special characters; enable two-factor authentication on every account that offers it; change passwords periodically; never use your date of birth, spouse's name, vehicle number or other easily guessable personal facts as a password.
Verifying a genuine deposit-taking NBFC: check that the NBFC's name appears in RBI's own published list of NBFCs authorised to accept deposits (at rbi.org.in) and does not appear on RBI's list of entities prohibited from taking deposits; a genuine deposit-taking NBFC must prominently display its RBI-issued Certificate of Registration (which must specifically show it is authorised to accept deposits); by rule, an NBFC cannot accept a deposit for less than 12 months or more than 60 months, and cannot pay more than 12.5% annual interest to a depositor — offers outside these bounds are a red flag.
Precautions for anyone depositing money with a bank/NBFC/company: always insist on a signed receipt for every deposit, stating the date, depositor's name, amount in words and figures, interest rate, and maturity date/amount; if a broker or agent is collecting the deposit on the NBFC's behalf, verify they are actually authorised by that NBFC to do so; remember that NBFC deposits are NOT covered by Deposit Insurance, unlike bank deposits.
Where to file a complaint: online complaints against RBI-regulated entities go to the RBI Ombudsman at cms.rbi.org.in (or by post to CRPC, Reserve Bank of India, Central Vista, Sector-17, Chandigarh-160017); securities-market complaints go to SEBI at sebi.gov.in; insurance complaints go to IRDAI at irdai.gov.in; housing-finance complaints go to the National Housing Bank at nhb.org.in; and any cybercrime, including financial fraud, should be reported to the Cyber Police via cybercrime.gov.in or helpline 1930.
Key term — Vishing: phone calls or social-media approaches pretending to be from a bank, non-bank e-wallet provider or telecom service provider, luring the customer into sharing confidential details under the pretext of KYC updation, unblocking an account or SIM, or crediting back a debited amount.
Key term — Phishing: spoofed emails or SMS messages designed to look like they came from the customer's own bank or e-wallet provider, containing links built to extract confidential details.
Key term — Remote Access fraud: luring a customer into downloading an app on their phone or computer that lets the fraudster access all of that customer's data and control the device remotely.
Key term — Money mule: a person, often an unwitting victim, who is exploited by fraudsters into laundering stolen or illegal money through their own bank account.
Key term — UPI (Unified Payments Interface): a platform for transferring money between bank/wallet accounts via an internet-connected mobile phone, authenticated by a UPI-PIN; the UPI-PIN is confidential and must never be shared with anyone, and is only ever needed to send money, never to receive it.
Key term — OTP (One Time Password): a confidential, single-use authentication code sent to the customer for carrying out an online transaction; it must never be shared with anyone, including bank staff, RBI officials, family or friends.
Key term — CVV: the 3-digit Card Verification Value printed on a card, required for most online transactions; it is confidential and must never be shared with anyone under any circumstance..
RBI Caution Directive (Press Release 2021-2022/1789): Banks, NBFCs, or e-wallet providers never ask customers for confidential details like user ID, login password, OTP, debit/credit card PIN, CVV, or expiry date over phone calls or SMS.
RBI KYC Updation Safety Rule: Any phone call or email threatening immediate blocking or suspension of your account on the pretext of non-updation of KYC is a fraud modus operandi. Legitimate KYC updates must only be accessed through official bank websites or physical branches.
RBI Customer Liability Guidelines (Master Direction DA.1/DA.3): In case of an unauthorized or fraudulent electronic banking transaction, customers must notify their bank immediately and lodge a complaint on the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal or National Cyber Crime Helpline (1930) at the earliest to limit financial liability.
RBI Remote Access & APK Warning: Members of the public are advised never to download unknown apps, third-party APKs, or remote control applications on mobile devices upon request by unknown callers, as these allow fraudsters secret access to confidential bank data.
RBI Digital Payment Rule (Directions 2021): Transactions involving the receipt of money into an account never require scanning barcodes, entering a UPI PIN, or entering an MPIN. UPI PIN is strictly required only for sending/debiting money.
